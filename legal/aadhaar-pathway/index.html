The Aadhaar Legal Pathway

The QueroCura Team · Last updated September 2026

This is the first question every hospital lawyer asks, so we're answering it in writing, in plain language, instead of waiting to be asked.

Emergency Glass Door matches a fingerprint or iris scan to a patient's identity so a care team can reach their emergency record in seconds, without the patient needing to be conscious enough to present a code or grant consent in the moment. That only works if the identity layer underneath it is built the way Aadhaar law requires — not just the way that's convenient.

What we store, and what we deliberately don't

Identity verification is designed to run on Aadhaar's offline, XML-based identity attributes and UIDAI-sanctioned biometric matching — not on holding a copy of the raw 12-digit Aadhaar number itself. That distinction is the whole compliance strategy: a system that never stores the number can't leak the number, and doesn't carry the same custodial obligations under Section 33 of the Aadhaar Act as a system that does.

Where we are today: this is the architecture Emergency Glass Door is designed around. We're publishing the commitment now, ahead of onboarding our first hospitals, so it can be reviewed — and held us to — before it matters in production. A hospital's security or legal team is welcome to request the specifics of our current implementation directly.

Section 33 and the DPDP Act, in plain terms

Section 33 of the Aadhaar Act restricts when Aadhaar numbers and authentication records can be disclosed, and to whom — bulk access and identification-at-scale are exactly what it's written to prevent. A break-glass emergency workflow has to satisfy that at the design level, not the policy level: matching a scan against a patient's biometric reference should never require pulling or exposing their Aadhaar number to hospital staff, the care team, or QueroCura itself.

The DPDP Act 2023 layers consent and purpose-limitation requirements on top of that. In an emergency, the patient often can't grant consent in the moment — which is precisely the scenario the law anticipates with narrow, purpose-bound exceptions for protecting life. Emergency Glass Door is scoped to exactly that purpose: identity verification to retrieve emergency-relevant medical data, nothing broader.

"A failed match looks identical to no record existing at all."

That non-disclosure property (detailed on our security page) does double duty here — it also means a scan can't be used to fish for whether a given person has any record with us at all, which is its own quiet answer to the bulk-search concern behind Section 33.

UIDAI's biometric lock

UIDAI lets any Aadhaar holder lock their biometrics, which blocks biometric authentication until they unlock it themselves. We respect that setting rather than working around it — if a patient has locked their biometrics, Emergency Glass Door will not authenticate against them. It's a real limitation of a biometric-first design, and we'd rather state it plainly than paper over it: a locked biometric means the care team falls back to whatever identification process the hospital already uses today.

NABH's two-point identification standard

NABH requires two independent points of patient identification before treatment. The common practical answer today — photo plus UHID — identifies the patient but says nothing about their medical history. A biometric match against a verified identity record satisfies the identification requirement and, on a successful match, closes the history gap in the same motion. It's designed to sit alongside a hospital's existing NABH-compliant identification step, not replace it.

Why this isn't redundant with ABDM

ABDM already supports Aadhaar-linked biometric authentication through ABDM-enabled HMIS at hospitals that have adopted it — which raises a fair question: why build a separate pathway at all? Two reasons. First, ABDM adoption in Indian hospitals is still early (single-digit percent of clinics, under a fifth of hospitals), so a break-glass workflow that depends entirely on a hospital already being ABDM-integrated leaves most of the country's emergency departments uncovered. Second, ABDM's consent architecture is built around a conscious patient granting scoped, revocable access — exactly the step an unresponsive or unidentified patient can't perform. Emergency Glass Door is designed to be the pathway for that specific gap, and to interoperate with ABDM-enabled HMIS rather than compete with it as hospitals adopt ABDM more broadly.

ABDM integration status: not yet certified. QueroCura does not currently hold Health Information User (HIU) status under ABDM, which means we cannot yet programmatically pull records from ABDM-linked hospitals or labs. That application is in progress. We're publishing this honestly rather than implying a certification we don't have — see our security page for the rest of our current compliance posture.
